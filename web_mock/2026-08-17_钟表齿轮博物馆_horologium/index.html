The Collection

Masterpieces across four eras of horology

From the first mechanical clocks of medieval monasteries to the exquisite complications of the Belle Époque, our holdings trace the evolution of humanity's quest to measure time.

Nürnberger Ei

Peter Henlein · c. 1510

The earliest surviving portable timepiece. A drum-shaped clock of gilt brass, driven by a mainspring and regulated by a verge escapement with foliot.

OriginNuremberg, Germany

Stackfreed Watch

Unknown · c. 1550

An early solution to mainspring torque variation. An eccentric cam applies increasing friction as the spring unwinds, improving rate accuracy.

MechanismStackfreed

Fusee Table Clock

Hans Gruber · c. 1580

A brass table clock employing the fusee — a conical pulley that equalizes mainspring force across the entire run, revolutionizing timekeeping precision.

Height28 cm

Huygens Pendulum Clock

Salomon Coster · 1657

The first pendulum clock built under Christiaan Huygens's patent. Reduced daily error from minutes to seconds — the greatest leap in horological precision.

Accuracy±15 sec/day

Anchor Escapement

William Clement · 1671

Invented by William Clement, the anchor escapement reduced recoil and required far less power than the verge, enabling longer pendulums and finer regulation.

Period1 second

Quarter Repeater Pocket Watch

Daniel Quare · c. 1700

A masterpiece of complication. Pressing the pendant triggers a sequence of hammers on tuned gongs, sounding the hours and quarters on demand.

ComplicationQuarter Repeater

Harrison H4

John Harrison · 1759

The marine chronometer that solved the longitude problem. A large pocket-watch-sized timekeeper that proved accurate enough to determine longitude at sea.

Accuracy±5 sec / voyage

Breguet Perpetuelle

Abraham-Louis Breguet · 1780

The first self-winding watch with a rotor weight. Breguet's "perpetual" winding system used an oscillating weight that wound the mainspring with the wearer's movement.

InnovationAutomatic winding

Tourbillon Escapement

Abraham-Louis Breguet · 1795

Breguet's revolutionary carriage rotates the entire balance and escapement once per minute, averaging out gravitational errors in pocket watches carried vertically.

Patent1801 · France

Grand Complication

Patek Philippe · 1898

A pocket watch combining minute repeater, perpetual calendar, moonphase, and chronograph — the apex of late 19th-century watchmaking virtuosity.

Parts650 components

Cartier Santos-Dumont

Louis Cartier · 1904

The first men's wristwatch, created for aviator Alberto Santos-Dumont. Its square case and exposed screws defined a century of watch design.

CaliberManual 1904 MC

Astronomical Regulator

Société Genevoise · 1910

A precision regulator with equation of time, sidereal time, and perpetual calendar — the ultimate observatory timepiece before electronic standards.

Precision±0.5 sec/day
The Craft

The watchmaker's hand — six disciplines of horological art

A mechanical timepiece is a universe of miniaturized mechanics. Each discipline below represents a lifetime of mastery, practiced by the few who can shape brass and steel into instruments that outlive generations.

01 / Complications

Complication Making

Designing and assembling mechanisms beyond simple timekeeping — repeaters, chronographs, perpetual calendars, and tourbillons. Each complication is a puzzle of levers, cams, and springs measured in hundredths of a millimeter.

02 / Finishing

Decorative Finishing

Côtes de Genève, perlage, anglage, guilloché — the art of rendering functional metal into something luminous. Surfaces the wearer never sees are still finished by hand, a discipline of invisible perfection.

03 / Regulation

Regulation & Timing

Adjusting the balance spring and escapement to achieve consistent rate across positions, temperatures, and power reserves. The regulator's ear and eye are the final judge of a movement's quality.

04 / Escapements

Escapement Design

The heart of the watch — the mechanism that releases the gear train in measured increments. From verge to co-axial, each design trades efficiency, friction, and isochronism in pursuit of perfect time.

05 / Enameling

Enamel Dial Making

Firing powdered glass onto copper at 800°C to create dials of luminous, unfading color. Grand feu, cloisonné, and miniature enamel — each a craft that can take a master years to learn and a week to execute one dial.

06 / Gearing

Gear Cutting

Cutting and depthing wheels and pinions with mathematically perfect tooth profiles. A single grain of dust in the wrong place can ruin weeks of work; a well-made gear train transmits power with near-zero loss.

The gear train — power in, precision out

A simple set of gears transforms the slow, forceful unwind of the mainspring into the rapid, measured tick of the escapement. Each wheel meshes with the next at mathematically calculated ratios, turning hours into seconds.

The Escapement

Where time becomes measurable

The escapement is the heartbeat of every mechanical clock. It gives the gear train permission to advance one tooth at a time — a measured release driven by the oscillations of the pendulum or balance wheel.

Each tick is the pallet stone unlocking one tooth, followed by a tiny impulse that keeps the pendulum swinging. Without this delicate exchange — locking, impulse, unlocking — the mainspring would unwind in seconds and the clock would race to a halt.
